A book for grandparents: gathering their story before it is lost

by Stefano Braccini, editorial coordinator · 16 November 2026 · all articles

Grandparents have seen a world that no longer exists: the village before the motorway, the stories of the war heard at home. And they have stories that are theirs alone: their first job, their first pay packet, the day they met. These are stories that you hear at the dinner table and that, unless someone writes them down, die with the person who tells them. A book holds them together, for the children and the grandchildren. Here is how to begin.

The right time is now

There is no need to wait for an anniversary: one afternoon a week is enough to begin, and every story recorded today is a story kept safe.

How to ask the questions

General questions (“what was your childhood like?”) bring general answers. The ones that work better ask about a day, a place, an object. A few examples to start from:

  1. What is the first house you remember? What was the kitchen like?
  2. What did you eat on Sundays, and who cooked?
  3. How did you meet Grandad, or Grandma? What struck you that day?
  4. What was your first job, and how much did you earn?
  5. Is there a day in your life you have never told anyone about?
  6. What would you like your grandchildren to know about you?

Listen without interrupting, and when a story comes alive, ask, “And then?” The best part often comes the second time a story is told.

Record, then transcribe

A phone is enough: you put it on the table and let the conversation flow. Recording lets you really listen, instead of taking notes, and it keeps something no book can hold: the voice. After each meeting, transcribe the best passages and note down on a sheet the names, dates and places to check.

Photos, letters, documents

Photographs open up memories better than any question: look at them together with your grandparents and ask who is there, where, in what year. Letters, postcards, school reports, handwritten recipes and work papers are precious material, and they can go into the book as images too.

From the recordings to the book

Here comes the hardest part: turning hours of stories into a text that reads from beginning to end. You have to choose an order (the years, the places, the people), cut the repetitions, link the episodes and keep the voice of the person telling them. The best book is the one in which the grandchildren, as they read, hear Grandad talking.

When we write the book

If time is short, or if there are many recordings and you do not know where to start, we can write it for you. If needed, we do the interviews by phone or video call, at an unhurried pace, and we also work from the recordings you already have; then we write in the first person, in your grandfather's or grandmother's voice, and the family reads and approves every chapter. If instead the text already exists (handwritten memoirs, a diary, a recipe notebook), we transcribe it, revise it while leaving the voice as it is, and lay it out with the family photographs.

There are three paths, and the price changes from one to another:

  1. The text exists (editing, layout, cover): a few weeks are enough.
  2. Short collection of memories, to be written.
  3. Biography with interviews, in the short or the full format: usually 3 to 6 months.
