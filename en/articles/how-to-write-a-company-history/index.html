How to write a company history, with examples

by Stefano Braccini, editorial coordinator · 16 November 2026 · all articles

Every company that lasts has a story: who founded it, the first premises, the difficult year, the product that changed everything. Told well, that story becomes a book to give to clients, to hand to employees, to leave to those who come after. Here is how to write it, step by step, with a few examples.

First of all: who the book is for

A book for an anniversary, aimed at clients and suppliers, is not the same as a book for a generational handover, which speaks above all to the family and the employees. Decide straight away who will read it: the tone, the length and how much room to give to figures and to people all depend on this.

The material: archives and interviews

A company's history lives in two places. The first is the archive: founding deeds, old photographs, catalogues, advertisements, press cuttings, letters to clients. Gather it all before you write and put it in chronological order. The second is the people: the founder or their children, the longest-serving employees, a long-standing client, a supplier. They are the ones who remember the episodes no document keeps, and it is the episodes that make the book worth reading.

How to structure the story

Three approaches work well:

  1. By period: one chapter for each decade or each phase of the company. It is the clearest, and it works well for anniversaries.
  2. By people: one chapter for each generation or each key figure. It works when the company is also a family story.
  3. By products or places: one chapter for each product that marked a turning point, or for each site. It works when the company recognises itself in what it does.

Opening examples

The beginning decides whether the book will be read or left on the shelf. Here are three ways to begin, with sentences invented for the occasion:

  1. A scene: “The first machine arrived on a Monday in March, on a borrowed lorry. Nobody yet knew how to make it work.”
  2. An object: “In the founder's office there is still the first sales ledger, with its green cover and thirty-two clients written in by hand.”
  3. A voice: “My father used to say that you judge a company by how it treats the customer who buys little.”

All three work better than “The company was founded in...”: the date still arrives, but only once the reader is already curious.

The mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is writing a list of turnover figures and openings: numbers matter, but on their own they say nothing. The second is hiding the difficult moments. A crisis overcome, a fire, a market lost and won back are often the strongest pages, because they show how the company reacted. The third is forgetting the people: someone who worked on the shop floor for thirty years deserves a name and a sentence, not just a group photo.
