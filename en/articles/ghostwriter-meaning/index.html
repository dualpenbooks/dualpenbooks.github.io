Ghostwriter: what it means and where the word comes from

by Stefano Braccini, editorial coordinator · 20 November 2026 · all articles

A ghostwriter is someone who writes a text that another person then publishes under their own name: a book, a speech, an article. Here is what the word means, where it comes from and what a ghostwriter actually does.

The meaning

The Merriam-Webster dictionary defines a ghostwriter as “one that writes for and in the name of another”. The Italian Treccani dictionary adds that a ghostwriter may also give finished form to ideas that someone else has only sketched out, and that is precisely what the job involves day to day.

The named author and the writer matter equally. The text comes out under the author's name, and usually the ideas, the memories and the expertise are theirs; the ghostwriter brings the craft, that is, the structure, the sentences and the rhythm.

Where the word comes from

It is an English compound: ghost and writer. Literally, a writer who is there but cannot be seen. Merriam-Webster dates the first known use of the verb “ghostwrite” (to write for someone else) to 1927, and describes it as a back-formation from the noun ghostwriter, which therefore already existed.

In France: from “nègre” to “prête-plume”

In French, someone who wrote on another person's behalf was long called a “nègre”, or “nègre littéraire”. The image was that of the slave: someone who does all the work without getting the credit. One of the most famous cases is that of Alexandre Dumas: in 1845 Eugène de Mirecourt attacked him in the pamphlet “Fabrique de romans”, accusing him of putting his name to novels written with his collaborators, chief among them Auguste Maquet.

Precisely because of that image, the word is now avoided. In 2017, after a petition and a request from the Representative Council of Black Associations of France, the French Ministry of Culture recommended using “prête-plume”, that is, someone who lends their pen. The dictionary of the Académie française, in its latest edition, also warns that the word has a racist connotation and recommends “prête-plume” instead.

How it is spelt

You will find three spellings: ghostwriter as one word, ghost writer as two words, and ghost-writer with a hyphen. Merriam-Webster gives ghostwriter as one word; the Italian Treccani dictionary records ghost-writer, with the hyphen.

What a ghostwriter does, in practice

Ghostwriters are best known for biographies, but the work is much broader. Commissioned writing includes technical and professional manuals, novels, business books, essays, speeches for a wedding or a ceremony, articles. The starting point is always the same: someone has a story or expertise, and does not have the time or the skill to put it into writing.

The work usually goes like this:

  1. Talking. The future author tells their story, by phone or video call, and the ghostwriter asks the right questions.
  2. Writing. The text takes shape one chapter at a time, in the author's voice, not the writer's.
  3. Approving. Every chapter goes to the author, who reads it, corrects it and approves it.

In the end the book belongs to the person whose name is on it: name on the cover, rights and earnings. The ghostwriter remains, quite literally, a ghost.

Ghostwriter, editor, co-author

The three roles are often confused. An editor usually works on a text that already exists: they read it, point out what does not work, suggest cuts and corrections. A co-author is credited alongside the author, and their name appears on the cover. A ghostwriter writes the text, wholly or largely, and as a rule their name does not appear.
