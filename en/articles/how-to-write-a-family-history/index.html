How to write a family history

by Stefano Braccini, editorial coordinator · 19 November 2026 · all articles

In every family there is someone who knows the names of the great-grandparents, the place the surname comes from, the story of the uncle who left for America. This is knowledge passed on by word of mouth from one generation to the next, and each time it is passed on, a piece goes missing. Writing your family's history means capturing it before it fades, and putting it in order for those who will come after you. Here is how to do it, one step at a time.

Decide how far back to go

The first choice is the scope. You can follow a single line, your father's or your mother's, or both; you can stop at your grandparents or go further back. Three generations are already enough for a book. A story told well is better than a list of names without faces: if you find more material later, the scope can widen.

Start from the family tree

Before you write, draw the tree with what you know: names, dates, places of birth, marriages. On a separate sheet, note the gaps, that is, the dates you do not know and the names nobody remembers. The tree is for you more than for the reader: it tells you whom to look for, what to ask and where the story needs a document.

Where to look for records

The first archive is the home: drawers, boxes in the loft, grandmother's chest of drawers. Look for letters, postcards, photographs with a date written on the back, passports, legal deeds, employment records, school reports. Then there are the archives outside the home:

  1. The register office: as a rule, a birth, marriage or death record is kept in the place where the event happened, and a copy can be requested there.
  2. State archives: in many countries the older civil registers are kept in public archives, and many can be consulted online. If your family comes from Italy, on the Portale Antenati of the Italian Ministry of Culture many civil registers held in the State Archives can be consulted online, free of charge.
  3. The parish: for the earliest periods, the registers of baptisms and marriages are often the only trace.
  4. Passenger lists: anyone who emigrated to the United States through Ellis Island often appears in the ships' lists, which can be consulted online.

Your relatives' voices

Records tell you when and where; people know why. Talk to your oldest relatives, and not only those on your side of the family: a distant cousin may have the letter that went missing in your own home. Record the conversations on your phone, so that you can listen instead of taking notes. Two relatives will sometimes tell the same story differently: do not choose straight away, write down both versions. Sometimes a document settles the matter; sometimes the real story lies precisely in those two memories that do not match.

How to structure the story

There are three approaches that work:

  1. By generation: one chapter for each pair of ancestors, from the most distant down to you. It is the clearest.
  2. By place: the place of origin, the city the family moved to, the foreign country someone emigrated to. It works when the family moved a great deal.
  3. Around a common thread: a house, a trade, a shop passed down from father to son. It works when something held the family together for decades.

As you write, keep documented facts and family stories apart: “the 1912 register shows...” and “in the family it was said that...” both have their place, as long as the reader always knows which of the two they are reading. And place the photographs next to the pages about the people in them.
